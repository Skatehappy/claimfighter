Auto Insurance Claim Denial Law in Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania protects auto insurance policyholders through several layered laws. The cornerstone is 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371, the bad faith statute, which allows a court to award interest at the prime rate plus 3%, punitive damages, and attorney's fees if an insurer acts in bad faith toward its insured. Pennsylvania courts apply the two-part test from Terletsky v. Prudential: the insured must show (1) the insurer lacked a reasonable basis for denying benefits, and (2) the insurer knew or recklessly disregarded that lack of reasonable basis. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court confirmed in Rancosky v. Washington National (2017) that ill will is not required, only knowing or reckless conduct. Beyond § 8371, the Unfair Insurance Practices Act (40 P.S. § 1171.5) and 31 Pa. Code § 146 prohibit specific conduct: failing to acknowledge a claim within 10 working days, failing to complete an investigation within 30 days of notice, refusing to pay without conducting a reasonable investigation, misrepresenting policy provisions, or compelling insureds to sue to recover amounts due. Although the UIPA does not create a direct private right of action, courts treat violations as strong evidence of bad faith. Pennsylvania also follows a choice no-fault system for auto insurance, meaning your right to sue and your benefits depend on whether you selected limited or full tort coverage. First-party medical benefits (PIP) must generally be paid within 30 days after the insurer receives reasonable proof, and overdue benefits accrue 12% interest under 75 Pa.C.S. § 1716. Statutory attorney's fees under 75 Pa.C.S. § 1798 may also apply for unreasonable refusal to pay PIP benefits.

How a Demand Letter Works in Pennsylvania

An effective Pennsylvania demand letter does more than complain about a denial; it builds a record that supports a future bad faith claim. Start by identifying the policy, claim number, date of loss, and the specific denial language used by the adjuster. Quote the policy provision the insurer relied on and explain why it does not apply to your facts. Then cite Pennsylvania's controlling authority: 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371, the Terletsky and Rancosky standards, and the relevant subsections of 31 Pa. Code § 146 the insurer appears to have violated (such as failing to investigate within 30 days or failing to provide a reasonable explanation for denial). If the claim involves first-party medical benefits, reference 75 Pa.C.S. §§ 1716 and 1798 and demand 12% interest plus attorney's fees. Attach supporting documents: repair estimates, medical bills, photographs, police reports, and witness statements. Set a firm response deadline (commonly 15 to 30 days) and state clearly that if the insurer does not reverse the denial or provide a written reasonable basis, you will pursue all available remedies, including punitive damages and attorney's fees under § 8371. Send the letter by certified mail, return receipt requested, and keep copies of everything. A clear, well-cited demand letter signals that you understand Pennsylvania law and are prepared to litigate, which often prompts insurers to reopen the file, request additional information, or settle.

Procedural Notes for Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania's small claims limit (Magisterial District Court) is $12,000, which can be useful for smaller property damage disputes. Filing fees vary by county but typically range from $60 to $150. For larger claims, file in the Court of Common Pleas. The statute of limitations is 4 years for contract-based claims and bad faith under § 8371, and 2 years for related tort claims. Before filing, you may also submit a complaint to the Pennsylvania Insurance Department (1-877-881-6388), which can pressure the insurer to reconsider. Note that insurers sometimes attempt to remove cases to federal court if diversity exists; this is common in § 8371 actions. Always check your specific policy for any contractual suit limitation periods, which Pennsylvania courts generally enforce if reasonable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What qualifies as bad faith by an auto insurer in Pennsylvania?
Under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371 and the Terletsky/Rancosky standard, bad faith means the insurer lacked a reasonable basis for denying or delaying your claim and knew or recklessly disregarded that fact. Examples include denying without investigation, ignoring favorable evidence, misrepresenting policy terms, lowballing settlement offers, or failing to communicate. You do not need to prove the insurer acted with hatred or ill will, only that it acted unreasonably and knowingly. Documenting every communication strengthens your case.
How long does my insurer have to respond to my claim in Pennsylvania?
Under 31 Pa. Code § 146, an insurer must acknowledge your claim within 10 working days, begin investigation promptly, and complete the investigation within 30 days of receiving notice unless it cannot reasonably do so. For first-party medical (PIP) benefits, payment is generally due within 30 days after the insurer receives reasonable proof of loss under 75 Pa.C.S. § 1716. Overdue PIP benefits accrue 12% interest, and unreasonable refusal can trigger attorney's fees.
Can I recover attorney's fees if I sue my auto insurer in Pennsylvania?
Yes, in two main situations. Under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371, a court may award attorney's fees, interest at prime plus 3%, and punitive damages if you prove bad faith. For first-party medical benefit disputes, 75 Pa.C.S. § 1798 allows attorney's fees when the insurer's refusal to pay was without reasonable foundation. These fee-shifting provisions make Pennsylvania a relatively pro-policyholder state and give a demand letter real leverage when properly drafted.
Should I file my dispute in small claims court or Common Pleas?
Pennsylvania's Magisterial District Courts handle claims up to $12,000, making them suitable for smaller property damage disputes where speed and low filing fees matter. For larger claims, claims involving bad faith damages under § 8371, or cases seeking punitive damages, file in the Court of Common Pleas. Magisterial District Courts cannot award the full range of bad faith remedies. Consider also filing a complaint with the Pennsylvania Insurance Department in parallel.
How long do I have to sue my auto insurer in Pennsylvania?
Bad faith claims under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371 generally have a 2-year statute of limitations as a tort, while breach of contract claims on the policy have a 4-year limit. Underinsured/uninsured motorist claims also follow the 4-year contract period, typically running from the date of denial or breach. However, your policy may contain a shorter contractual suit limitation, which Pennsylvania courts enforce if reasonable. Always check your policy and act quickly to preserve your rights.
