Auto Insurance Claim Denial Law in Illinois

Illinois regulates auto insurance claim handling primarily through two statutes: 215 ILCS 5/154.6, which lists improper claims practices, and 215 ILCS 5/155, which provides the remedy when an insurer acts vexatiously and unreasonably. Section 154.6 prohibits insurers from denying claims without conducting a reasonable investigation, failing to acknowledge communications promptly, refusing to pay claims without explaining the policy basis, compelling policyholders to litigate by offering substantially less than is owed, and misrepresenting policy provisions. While Section 154.6 does not by itself create a private lawsuit, it defines the conduct that supports a Section 155 claim. Under 215 ILCS 5/155, if an insurer's denial, delay, or settlement conduct is found vexatious and unreasonable, the court may award the policyholder attorney fees plus a penalty equal to the lesser of: (1) 60% of the amount the court finds owed, (2) $60,000, or (3) the difference between what the insurer offered and what the court awards. These remedies are in addition to the actual benefits owed under the policy. Illinois also recognizes common-law breach of contract claims against insurers, and in some circumstances claims under the Illinois Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act (815 ILCS 505/) may apply. The Illinois Department of Insurance also accepts consumer complaints and can pressure insurers to reconsider denials. Importantly, Illinois courts construe ambiguous policy language against the insurer, meaning a borderline coverage dispute often tilts in the policyholder's favor. Most Illinois auto policies contain a contractual suit limitation of one or two years from the date of loss, so acting quickly is essential to preserve your rights.

How a Demand Letter Works in Illinois

An effective Illinois demand letter does more than complain about a denial; it builds a record that supports a future Section 155 claim. Start by identifying the policy, claim number, date of loss, and the insurer's specific denial language. Then quote the policy provisions that actually cover your loss and explain why the insurer's stated reason is wrong or unsupported. Attach key evidence: the police report, repair estimates, photographs, medical records if applicable, and any prior correspondence. Cite 215 ILCS 5/154.6 to put the insurer on notice that its conduct may constitute an improper claims practice, and cite 215 ILCS 5/155 to signal that you are prepared to seek statutory penalties, attorney fees, and costs if the denial is not reversed. Set a clear deadline, typically 14 to 30 days, for the insurer to pay or provide a written explanation grounded in the policy. State that you will file a complaint with the Illinois Department of Insurance and pursue litigation if necessary. Tone matters: a professional, fact-driven letter is far more persuasive than an emotional one. Send the letter by certified mail with return receipt requested, and keep copies of everything. Many Illinois insurers reverse questionable denials at this stage because the cost of defending a Section 155 action, including potential fee shifting, often exceeds the disputed claim amount. Even if the insurer does not pay in full, a strong demand letter frequently produces an improved offer or a more detailed coverage position that helps you prepare for the next step.

Procedural Notes for Illinois

Illinois small claims court handles disputes up to $10,000 and is available in each county's Circuit Court under Illinois Supreme Court Rules 281-289. Filing fees vary by county, generally ranging from about $75 to $250. For larger disputes, you must file in the regular civil division. Most Illinois auto policies impose a one or two year contractual limitation period for filing suit, which can be shorter than the standard ten-year contract statute of limitations, so review your policy carefully. You may also file a free complaint with the Illinois Department of Insurance, which often prompts a written response from the insurer within 21 days. Venue is typically proper where the policyholder resides or where the loss occurred.

Your Rights as an Illinois Policyholder

Claim-handling deadlines: In Illinois, your insurer must acknowledge your claim within 15 working days after receiving notice or proof of loss (50 Ill. Adm. Code 919.40), accept or deny it within a reasonable time after completing its investigation (50 Ill. Adm. Code 919.50), and pay an accepted claim promptly once the amount is affirmed and undisputed. If a claim remains unresolved, the insurer must send a written explanation of delay every 45 days (50 Ill. Adm. Code 919.80). There is no separate statutory interest rate for property claims; the 215 ILCS 5/155 penalty is the primary consequence of unreasonable delay.

Bad-faith remedies: Illinois recognizes statutory remedy only — Illinois does not recognize a separate common-law bad-faith tort. 215 ILCS 5/155 is the exclusive first-party remedy for vexatious and unreasonable claim handling; Illinois courts do not recognize an independent common-law bad-faith tort in first-party cases. A court may award reasonable attorney's fees and costs plus an additional penalty — the greater of 60% of the amount the court finds owed, $60,000, or the amount by which the claim exceeds the insurer's pre-suit offer (215 ILCS 5/155).

Appraisal rights: Illinois property policies commonly include an appraisal clause, and courts treat amount-of-loss and valuation disputes as appraisal-eligible once coverage is admitted.

Deadline to sue: Written-contract actions have a 10-year limitations period (735 ILCS 5/13-206), but most Illinois property policies impose a valid contractual suit-limitation, often 1 year from the date of loss, which the courts generally enforce.

Health-claim appeals: For health claims, the Health Carrier External Review Act (215 ILCS 180) requires you to exhaust internal appeals, then request external review generally within 4 months of the final adverse determination, with the independent reviewer's decision binding on the carrier.

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes an auto insurance denial 'vexatious and unreasonable' in Illinois?
Illinois courts look at the totality of the circumstances. Conduct that may qualify includes denying a claim without investigating, ignoring evidence supporting coverage, misrepresenting policy terms, demanding unnecessary documentation, or making lowball offers far below the actual loss. A genuine, good-faith dispute over coverage usually does not qualify, but a denial unsupported by the policy or facts often does. Section 155 of the Illinois Insurance Code allows penalties up to $60,000 plus attorney fees when this standard is met.
How long do I have to sue my auto insurer in Illinois?
Most Illinois auto policies contain a contractual limitation period of one or two years from the date of loss for first-party claims. This is typically shorter than Illinois's ten-year statute of limitations for written contracts. Read your policy carefully and note the exact deadline. For third-party bodily injury claims, the general personal injury statute of limitations is two years. Missing these deadlines can permanently bar your recovery, so send your demand letter and consult counsel promptly.
Can I file a complaint with the Illinois Department of Insurance?
Yes. The Illinois Department of Insurance accepts free consumer complaints online or by mail. Once filed, the Department typically requires the insurer to respond in writing within about 21 days. While the Department cannot force payment or award damages, its involvement frequently prompts insurers to reconsider questionable denials. Filing a complaint also creates an official record that supports a later Section 155 claim by showing the insurer was on notice of your dispute.
Will small claims court work for my Illinois auto claim?
Illinois small claims court handles disputes up to $10,000, making it suitable for many vehicle damage and minor injury claims. The process is faster and less formal than regular civil court, and you can represent yourself, although individuals suing in small claims may need an attorney if a corporation is involved. Filing fees are modest. For larger claims, including significant medical expenses or total loss disputes, you will need to file in the regular civil division of the Circuit Court.
Do I need a lawyer to send a demand letter in Illinois?
No, you can send a demand letter yourself, and a clearly written, well-supported letter often resolves the dispute without an attorney. However, if your claim is large, complex, or involves serious injuries, consulting an Illinois insurance attorney can significantly strengthen your position. Many attorneys handle bad faith insurance cases on a contingency basis, and Section 155 allows recovery of attorney fees, which means representation may cost less out of pocket than you expect.

What can I recover if my Illinois insurer acted in bad faith?
