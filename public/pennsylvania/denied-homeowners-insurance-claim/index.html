Denied Homeowners Insurance Claim Law in Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania protects homeowners through two main legal frameworks. The first is 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371, the state's bad faith statute. It allows policyholders to sue insurers who handle claims unreasonably, and courts can award interest at the prime rate plus 3%, punitive damages, and attorney fees. To prove bad faith, you must show the insurer lacked a reasonable basis for denying your claim and either knew or recklessly disregarded that lack of basis. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court confirmed in Rancosky v. Washington National Insurance Co. (2017) that you do not have to prove the insurer acted with self-interest or ill will, only the two elements above.

The second framework is the Unfair Insurance Practices Act (40 P.S. § 1171.1 et seq.) and its companion regulations at 31 Pa. Code § 146, which set specific timelines. Insurers must acknowledge a claim within 10 working days, begin investigation within 10 working days, and accept or deny the claim within 15 working days after receiving proof of loss (with extensions allowed if the insurer notifies you in writing every 30 days). Misrepresenting policy terms, failing to adopt reasonable standards for investigation, refusing to pay without reasonable investigation, or compelling lawsuits by offering substantially less than amounts ultimately recovered are all unfair practices.

Pennsylvania also recognizes common law breach of contract claims for denied homeowners policies. Homeowners can pursue contract damages alongside statutory bad faith claims. Importantly, the bad faith statute applies only to first-party insurance disputes, which includes virtually all homeowners claims where you are seeking benefits under your own policy.

How a Demand Letter Works in Pennsylvania

A strong Pennsylvania demand letter does several things at once. First, it documents the facts: the policy number, date of loss, type of damage, the amount claimed, and the insurer's denial reasoning. Second, it identifies specific statutory and regulatory violations, citing 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371 and the relevant subsections of 31 Pa. Code § 146 the insurer breached, such as failing to investigate promptly or denying without a reasonable basis. Third, it puts the insurer on notice that you understand the remedies available, including punitive damages and attorney fees, which often dwarf the underlying claim.

The letter should attach key evidence: photos, repair estimates from licensed contractors, the policy declarations, prior correspondence, and any expert reports. It should set a firm deadline, typically 15 to 30 days, for the insurer to reverse its denial or make a reasonable settlement offer. The letter should also request the complete claim file and adjuster notes, which Pennsylvania courts have allowed in discovery on bad faith claims.

Tone matters. A professional, fact-driven letter signals you are prepared to litigate, which carriers take seriously given Pennsylvania's bad faith exposure. Avoid emotional language and stick to documented losses, policy provisions, and statutory rights. Many homeowners' disputes settle after a credible demand letter because the insurer's downside risk, prime-rate-plus-3% interest accruing from the date of bad faith conduct plus potential punitive damages and attorney fees, often exceeds the cost of paying the claim in full.

Procedural Notes for Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania's Magisterial District Courts handle small claims up to $12,000, with filing fees typically ranging from $60 to $180 depending on the amount in controversy. Bad faith claims under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371 generally must be filed in the Court of Common Pleas because they often involve damages exceeding small claims limits and require statutory remedies the magisterial courts cannot fully award. The statute of limitations is four years for breach of contract and two years for bad faith claims under § 8371 (per Ash v. Continental Insurance Co.). Always check your specific policy for any contractual suit limitation period, often one or two years from date of loss. Venue is proper where the property is located or where the insurer does business.

Your Rights as a Pennsylvania Policyholder

Claim-handling deadlines: In Pennsylvania, your insurer must acknowledge your claim within 10 working days of receiving notice of the claim (31 Pa. Code § 146.5(a)), accept or deny it within 15 working days after receiving a properly executed proof of loss (31 Pa. Code § 146.7(a)), and pay an accepted claim promptly after acceptance once the amount is determined and undisputed. If the investigation is not complete within 30 days, the insurer must send a written delay explanation and update it every 45 days (31 Pa. Code § 146.6). Under the bad-faith statute, a court may award interest on the claim from the date it was made at the prime rate plus 3% (42 Pa.C.S. § 8371).

Bad-faith remedies: Pennsylvania recognizes statutory first-party bad faith. 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371 gives policyholders a statutory bad-faith cause of action; the Pennsylvania Supreme Court clarified the standard in Rancosky v. Washington National Ins. Co. (2017), adopting the two-part Terletsky test. A court may award interest on the claim at the prime rate plus 3%, punitive damages, and court costs and attorney's fees (42 Pa.C.S. § 8371).

Appraisal rights: Most Pennsylvania homeowners and auto policies contain an appraisal clause allowing either party to demand appraisal of an amount-of-loss dispute after a covered event.

Deadline to sue: First-party property (contract) claims have a 4-year limitations period (42 Pa.C.S. § 5525) and statutory bad-faith claims a 2-year period (42 Pa.C.S. § 5524), while many policies also recite a 1-year suit-limitation from the date of loss.

Health-claim appeals: For health claims, Act 68 gives you an internal grievance right (generally filed within 180 days) and Act 146 of 2022 established a Pennsylvania Insurance Department-administered external review by an independent review organization, with standard decisions typically in 45 days and expedited decisions in 72 hours.

Frequently Asked Questions

What qualifies as bad faith by my Pennsylvania insurer?
Bad faith includes denying a claim without a reasonable basis, failing to investigate properly, misrepresenting policy terms, delaying payment unreasonably, lowballing settlement offers, or ignoring evidence you submitted. Under Rancosky v. Washington National, you must show the insurer lacked a reasonable basis for its conduct and knew or recklessly disregarded that fact. You do not have to prove malice or ill will. Examples include denying a roof claim without inspecting the property or refusing to honor a clear policy provision.
How long does my insurer have to respond to my claim in Pennsylvania?
Under 31 Pa. Code § 146, your insurer must acknowledge receipt of your claim within 10 working days, begin investigating within 10 working days, and accept or deny the claim within 15 working days after receiving a proof of loss. If more time is needed, the insurer must notify you in writing every 30 days explaining the delay. Failure to meet these timelines can support a bad faith claim and unfair claims practices allegation.
Can I recover attorney fees if I sue my homeowners insurer?
Yes. Pennsylvania's bad faith statute, 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371, allows courts to award attorney fees, court costs, interest at the prime rate plus 3%, and punitive damages if you prove the insurer acted in bad faith. This is unusual because most American litigation requires each side to pay its own fees. The fee-shifting provision is precisely why demand letters citing § 8371 are effective: insurers face significant exposure beyond the claim amount itself.
What is the deadline to sue after a denied homeowners claim?
Pennsylvania applies a four-year statute of limitations to breach of contract claims and a two-year limit to statutory bad faith claims under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8371. However, your policy itself may impose a shorter contractual suit limitation, often one or two years from the date of loss, which Pennsylvania courts generally enforce. Read your policy carefully and act quickly. Sending a demand letter does not stop these deadlines from running.
Should I file in small claims court for a denied homeowners claim?
Pennsylvania Magisterial District Courts hear claims up to $12,000, which may work for smaller losses like limited water damage or theft. However, most homeowners claims, especially involving structural damage, fire, or major storm losses, exceed this limit and belong in the Court of Common Pleas. Bad faith claims seeking punitive damages and attorney fees under § 8371 generally require Common Pleas jurisdiction. Consult a Pennsylvania attorney to determine the right venue for your specific situation.

Code § 146.5(a)) and accept or deny it within 15 working days after receiving a properly executed proof of loss (31 Pa. Code § 146.7(a)). Under the bad-faith statute, a court may award interest on the claim from the date it was made at the prime rate plus 3% (42 Pa.C.S. § 8371).
What can I recover if my Pennsylvania insurer acted in bad faith?
A court may award interest on the claim at the prime rate plus 3%, punitive damages, and court costs and attorney's fees (42 Pa.C.S. § 8371).
How long do I have to sue my insurer for a denied claim in Pennsylvania?
